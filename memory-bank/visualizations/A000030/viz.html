OEIS · A000030

Same rule, two fates

? Problem

Take the leading digit of every whole number from 1 to 999, and count how often each digit (1 through 9) shows up first. Every count comes out to exactly 111 — the same, flat 11.1% for every digit.

Does this stay flat as we count further, or was 999 just a convenient place to stop?

↓

1 Keep counting — it never settles

1a

Track just digit 1's share as the count goes further: at N=999 it's the 11.1% above. Push past the next power of ten and it lurches; push past the one after that and it swings back.

There is no limit here. However far you count, the next power of ten sends the share somewhere else again — this is not a slow approach to a fixed value, it oscillates forever.

↓

2 Why: where the numbers actually sit

2a

Heavily skewed toward the high end. The bins aren't visited equally often, so neither are the digits they decide — and nothing evens this out as you count further, which is exactly the oscillation in step 1.

↓

3 Same rule, a different input

3a

Apply the exact same "take the leading digit" rule to the powers of two (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, … — OEIS A008952) instead of the plain counting numbers. Frequencies among the first million powers:

dashed line marks Benford's law's predicted share for that digit — observed and predicted line up on every one.

These bars settle and stay settled — pushing the count further doesn't move them, unlike step 1.

↓

4 Why this one settles

4a

The leading digit of 2n is decided by the fractional part of n·log10(2). The same 10 bins, for n = 1..1,000,000:

Dead uniform — every bin gets almost exactly 10%. This is the equidistribution theorem: because log10(2) is irrational, its multiples never repeat a pattern and fill every bin equally. Step 2's skew is what breaks Benford's law for plain counting; this uniformity is what lets it hold for powers of two.

↓

One digit-extraction rule, two different inputs, two different fates:
